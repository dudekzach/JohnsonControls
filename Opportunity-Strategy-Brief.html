Section 1

Executive Readout

Read this first. Everything below supports these conclusions.

Bottom line

This document is presented as a voice self-service POC. It reads as the controlled first step of a platform evaluation. A single self-service use case (System Test-On) sits alongside requests for manual, automatic and predictive outbound dialing, chat and SMS self-service recommendations, containment reporting and operational dashboards. Those asks do not belong to a voicebot bakeoff. They belong to a CCaaS comparison.

Second conclusion, equally important: the "current-state technical baseline" is a lightly de-branded Omilia design specification. Omilia product terminology, node numbering, Visio flow exports, web-service definitions and even unresolved editorial comments about neutralizing that terminology survive in the file we were sent. We are being asked to reproduce a working incumbent application, on paper, against a spec written by its designer. Parity is table stakes. The win comes from compressing the design, not matching it.

Preliminary posture: pursue, qualify hard. Technically winnable and a good fit for Genesys Cloud CX. The strategic risk is not losing the POC — it is winning a bot-only POC that leaves NICE as the platform of record and Genesys as a subcontracted speech layer.

What the POC is fundamentally testing

Whether a vendor can rebuild Johnson Controls' conversational voice front door on its own platform and operate it against JCI's alarm-monitoring back end: open-ended intent capture and call steering, FAQ announcements, caller identification and verification, the System Test-On workflow with its eligibility rules, error and containment handling, transfer with context back to the contact center platform, and reporting — in English and Spanish (POC SOW §2, §12).

Underneath that stated test sit three unstated ones: can the vendor deliver without the APIs being ready; can the vendor simplify a design that has become expensive to maintain; and does the vendor's platform cover ground the current two-vendor stack does not.

The five things the AE should understand immediately

  1. We are responding to the incumbent's own design document. The baseline preserves Omilia constructs — miniApps, reference nodes (0100, 0510, 1044, 1070), OCP Console, the full prompt matrix, every web-service contract. Two review comments were never cleaned up, one asking to neutralize the miniApp language and the reply explaining the original Visio diagrams could not be recreated in time. Practical effect: whoever writes the most literal parity response looks safest to a technical reviewer, and Omilia looks safest of all. Our response has to reframe the evaluation criteria, not just satisfy them.
  2. The architecture question is unresolved, and it decides the size of this deal. The document assumes the contact center platform stays and the vendor plugs into it (baseline §5: SIP via NICE virtual number, RTP to the bot platform, NICE Studio scripts, screen pop on return). If we answer inside that frame, our ceiling is a self-service attach behind a competitor's CCaaS. If we propose Genesys Cloud CX as the voice front door with transfer to NICE during coexistence, we compete for the platform. Same POC, two entirely different opportunities. The AE needs to choose deliberately, and early.
  3. Every API in the document is unbuilt or undisclosed. Eight services are specified — SiteSearch, ContactSearch, ValidatePin, GetSystemsBySiteNo, GetSiteSystemOption, GetUnclearedSystemTestsBySiteNo, PlaceOnTest — and each carries "URL: To be provided later." Credentials are placeholders, and the API username in the document is literally omiliaapi. Any POC schedule we quote is hostage to JCI IT unless we bring our own mock service layer.
  4. Spanish is new scope, not parity. The baseline states supported languages as US English with full NLU. The POC requires the System Test-On journey in English and Spanish, with localized prompts, Spanish recognition, bilingual test coverage and per-language reporting (POC SOW §2, §9, §12). Competitors who treat this as a checkbox will underscope it. It is also one of the cleanest places to show a structural advantage rather than a feature.
  5. Outbound and digital are the consolidation tell — and our only platform-level surface in this document. Outbound dialing appears in scope, integration requirements, deliverables, success criteria and the vendor checklist. Chat and SMS appear as a required recommendation. Nothing about System Test-On needs a predictive dialer. Someone in JCI's evaluation team is measuring breadth.

Preliminary opportunity assessment

  • Fit: strong. Intent capture, ID&V, API-driven eligibility logic, bilingual voice, transfer with context, native reporting, outbound and digital in one platform — this maps cleanly to Genesys Cloud CX with AI Studio and Architect. SA view
  • Qualification: incomplete. No named sponsor, no business case, no volumes, no decision process, no timeline, no competitive set, no stated JCI Genesys footprint anywhere in the document. This is an SOW, not a qualified opportunity.
  • Effort: the System Test-On rule set is the real work — primary/secondary/unmonitored system resolution, fire-alarm classification codes, uncleared-test overlap, date-window validation in site time zone. Underestimating this is the most likely cause of a POC that slips or demos thin.
  • Recommended stance: compete for the platform decision, deliver the POC narrowly and exceptionally, and refuse to be scoped as a bot vendor.
Section 2

What the Customer Is Evaluating

The POC in business and solution terms, not a restatement of the requirement list.

Core use cases

One journey carries the evaluation. System Test-On lets an authorized caller place an alarm system or site into test mode so maintenance or testing can occur without normal alarm handling being triggered (POC SOW §9). It is a high-volume, low-value, rules-heavy call that JCI clearly wants off the agent desktop — and one where a wrong outcome has real consequences, because an incorrectly suppressed alarm is a safety and liability event, not a bad customer experience.

Around that journey sit four supporting capabilities: open-ended intent capture with disambiguation and routing; static FAQ announcements with repeat / new task / close handling; caller identification and verification; and transfer to a live agent with captured context. Outbound dialing and chat/SMS are positioned as demonstrate-or-describe, not build.

Required customer experience

  • Speech-first. Keypad accepted only where explicitly required or allowed, with defined error handling when a caller presses keys anyway (POC SOW §4).
  • Conversational, jargon-free, concise prompts; confirmation wherever data accuracy matters before submission.
  • Repeat, start over, new task and close intents supported where applicable.
  • Containment attempted, then honored. When a caller asks for an agent, the assistant may attempt to help once, then must transfer at configured thresholds or when business rules require it. The current-state threshold is two agent requests.
  • Explicit fallback for silence, unclear speech, invalid DTMF, invalid values and integration failure.

Integration expectations

  • Inbound call delivery from the contact center platform, and return transfer to it (POC SOW §7).
  • Consumption of REST or equivalent services to authenticate, search, validate and update site test status.
  • Token-based API authentication, secure credential handling, protection of sensitive data.
  • Configurable endpoints, payloads, response mapping, retry behavior, timeout handling and failure routing.
  • Captured values and derived context passed back for agent screen pop, routing, reporting and post-call analysis.

Success criteria — and the gap in them

Eleven acceptance areas are defined: call entry, intent recognition, authentication, System Test-On, bilingual System Test-On, transfer handoff, self-service exit, error handling, reporting, outbound dialing, chat and SMS (POC SOW §12). Every one is demonstration-based. Not one carries a number. There is no target containment rate, no intent-accuracy threshold, no completion-rate goal, no latency budget.

Why this matters

Unquantified criteria get scored on impression and on how closely a response resembles what the reviewer already knows. That favors the incumbent. Co-authoring measurable criteria with JCI — intent accuracy on a shared utterance set, Test-On completion rate, per-language parity, containment — is both a genuine service to them and a way to move the scoring onto ground we can win. SA recommendation

Customer responsibilities and dependencies

JCI owns non-production API access, test credentials, test data and integration documentation; routing and handoff requirements; representative intents, FAQs and approved prompt language; approval of business rules, exception logic, test cases and success criteria; and participation in design reviews, testing, triage and evaluation (POC SOW §11). The assumptions section states plainly that APIs, credentials, network access, sample data and routing paths will be available before build begins (POC SOW §13). Treat that as the single most important commitment in the document and get it dated.

Explicitly out of scope

  • Production deployment of anything, including multilingual, outbound, chat and SMS, unless separately agreed.
  • Additional self-service use cases beyond System Test-On.
  • Redesign or replacement of upstream or downstream enterprise systems.
  • Production security certification, performance tuning, DR and the operational support model.
  • Historical data migration and incumbent platform configuration migration.

That last exclusion is worth reading twice. JCI has pre-decided that nothing from the current implementation gets migrated. Whoever wins rebuilds from requirements. That removes the incumbent's usual switching-cost argument and it is a point the AE should hold onto.

Section 3

Current-State Readout

What the baseline tells us about the environment, and what actually has to be preserved.

NICE Contact Center Platform

NICE owns the call. It answers, treats and routes; it hands audio to the bot platform over SIP using a NICE virtual number; it receives the JSON result and delivers audio plus screen pop to the agent (baseline §5.1–5.3). Integration is script-level: download NICE Studio scripts, import them, update with the bot application ID.

SA view Two implications. First, the integration pattern JCI knows is "bot as an attached leg," so a Genesys-fronted architecture will need to be positioned deliberately rather than assumed. Second, this seam is where JCI's operational pain concentrates — two consoles, two reporting models, a hand-built JSON contract carrying eighteen fields, and no single system of record for the interaction. Every one of those is an argument for consolidation, and none of them requires us to criticize NICE.

Omilia conversational voice implementation

The application is assembled from typed miniApps — Intent, Announcement, YesNo, Numeric, Alphanumeric, Amount, Date, Entity, WebService, and Intelli for JavaScript business logic — composed into subdialog flows (baseline §1.1, §3.3). It works. It is also a rigid state machine whose behavior lives in configuration spread across dozens of numbered nodes.

The maintenance cost shows in the prompt matrix. Nearly every collection step carries an initial prompt, a first-error rephrase, a second-error rephrase, an agent-request reaction, an agent-request first-error rephrase, plus weighted error reactions at 40/30/30. For four ID&V fields and roughly a dozen Test-On steps, that is well over a hundred authored strings — before Spanish doubles it. Adding a use case means authoring another set. Adding a language means authoring them all again.

Read this as the real "why change"

Nothing in the document says JCI is unhappy. But a company satisfied with its conversational platform does not put a single self-service journey out to a rebuild POC, exclude configuration migration, add a second language, and ask about dialers. The likely driver is cost-plus-agility on a two-vendor stack. Validate with AE

Authentication and verification — name it accurately

The document calls this authentication. Functionally it is identification by account lookup, followed by authorization. The caller is located via ANI against SiteSearch, falling back to ContactSearch to obtain a site number, then to spoken central station number, then phone number, then street number plus zip code (baseline §4.3). None of those factors is a secret. The actual authorization check is the PIC — the personal identification code, spelled letter by letter, validated by ValidatePin, which returns both a validity flag and a pin_on_test_flag indicating whether that contact is authorized to place a test.

Two consequences. The verification factor is the PIC, and it is spoken. It appears in the audio, in the transcript, and — per baseline §5.3 — in the JSON posted back to the contact center platform as a field named PIC, alongside IsPicValid. That is a sensitive-data handling conversation we should raise before they do, and a place where platform-native secure handling is a credible differentiator. Validate current Genesys Cloud secure-data and recording-redaction capabilities before positioning

System Test-On complexity — the part everyone will underestimate

Between "yes, put my system on test" and submission sit a series of eligibility gates, each a separate API call:

  • Active alarm check. in_alarm_system_no non-null on any site lookup means transfer, immediately.
  • Primary / secondary / unmonitored resolution via GetSystemsBySiteNo plus GetSiteSystemOption on SYSTEM_UDF1. Two primaries, two secondaries or all-unmonitored transfer. One primary plus one unmonitored proceeds on the primary. Primary plus secondary proceeds on both. More than five candidate systems transfers.
  • Fire-alarm classification via GetSiteSystemOption on SYSTEM_UDF2, where the returned code decides everything: BAHVAC, BAONLY, CSONLY, METSYS, N/A and SUONLY proceed; Combo, Combo+, FAHVAC and FAONLY transfer.
  • Uncleared tests via GetUnclearedSystemTestsBySiteNo, returning zone lists, alarm-group lists, service-type lists and only/except modifiers — driving full-versus-partial test confirmation and override prompts.
  • Date and time validation. Start must be within 30 days of current date or the call transfers; start time may be up to five minutes in the past; all times are site-local, in the site's time zone; submission uses UNIX effective and expire dates with a testcat_id of 1 for customer test, 7 for future-dated, 2 for technician test.

This is not conversational design. It is deterministic, auditable, safety-relevant business logic — and it must be preserved exactly. It also happens to be the strongest argument against a purely generative approach to the whole journey, which is a conversation we should lead rather than have led to us.

Error handling and containment

Thresholds are explicit (baseline §4.5): three consecutive no-inputs, three no-matches, three mixed, six global mixed, three disconfirmations, three invalid DTMF at the intent step, two continuous agent requests, two global agent requests. Turn-taking is a five-second timeout with barge-in enabled everywhere (§4.6). These numbers are cheap for us to match and they are the kind of detail a technical reviewer checks, so match them and say so.

Operational and reporting expectations

The POC asks for interaction outcomes, intents, transfers, containment, API result status and self-service completion status, plus dashboards in the vendor checklist. The baseline offers no evidence of how any of this is reported today. Reasonable inference: it is stitched from bot-platform logs and NICE reporting. SA view Single-platform analytics is one of our least contested advantages here, and it costs the customer nothing to verify during the POC.

Section 5

POC Win Strategy

How to win the evaluation without rebuilding the incumbent's application for them.

What the POC must prove

  1. Genesys completes the System Test-On happy path end to end, in English and Spanish, against the documented service contracts.
  2. Genesys honors every eligibility rule exactly — active alarm, primary/secondary/unmonitored, fire-alarm codes, uncleared tests, date window — including the transfer outcomes.
  3. Open-ended intent capture routes representative utterances correctly, including disambiguation of vague responses.
  4. Transfer carries context to the agent, with the agreed fields and reason codes present on screen.
  5. Error, no-input, no-match, invalid-DTMF, retry-exhaustion and API-failure paths behave to the documented thresholds.
  6. Reporting comes out of the platform natively — intents, containment, transfers, API status, completion, split by language.

Where to be exceptional

  • The exception paths. Anyone can demo a happy path. Show a caller whose site is in alarm, a caller with an FAONLY fire panel, a caller with an existing partial test, a caller requesting a start date 45 days out. Then show the agent receiving the call already knowing why. That is the demo the evaluation team will remember.
  • Bilingual in one journey. Same rules, same integrations, same reporting, language as a dimension. Then show the change-once, applies-everywhere behavior when a rule changes.
  • The transfer moment. The context contract is still To Be Determined in their document. Arrive with a proposed field set and show the screen pop working. Turning their open item into our artifact is worth more than a feature.
  • Native reporting, live. Run the test scenarios, then open the analytics and show the outcomes without exporting anything.

Table stakes — meet these precisely and move on

Inbound call entry with DNIS, ANI and hours-of-operation metadata; FAQ announcements with repeat, new task and close; agent-request containment at the documented thresholds; speech-first with defined DTMF error handling; confirmation before submission; the documented error counters and five-second timeouts with barge-in. Match the numbers, state that you matched them, spend no demo time on them.

How to avoid rebuilding Omilia

Framing to use throughout the response

"We built to the business requirements in your specification, not to its implementation. Here is a traceability matrix from your reference nodes to our design, and here is where we did it in fewer moving parts." That single artifact lets a technical reviewer verify parity without forcing us into their structure — and it makes our simplification visible instead of suspicious. SA recommendation

  • Do not adopt miniApp vocabulary or node numbering in our design. Map to it once, in the matrix.
  • Do not reproduce the per-node prompt matrix. Show fewer authored artifacts as a stated improvement, with the same coverage.
  • Do not accept the ID&V ladder as fixed. Propose a shorter path, keep the current one as the fallback, let JCI choose.
  • Do state clearly which requirements we support as configured, which need design work, and which we recommend doing differently — §14 explicitly asks for this and most vendors will answer it defensively. Answering it honestly is a differentiator.

What to defer rather than over-engineer

  • Outbound and digital: demonstrate capability, do not build campaigns or channels into the POC. The document permits describe-or-demonstrate. Take it.
  • FAQ breadth: a representative handful proves the pattern. The full intent list is not even in the document.
  • Production hardening: security certification, DR, performance tuning and the support model are explicitly out of scope (§3). Address them in a production-readiness section, not in the build.
  • Prompt polish and voice selection: agree persona traits, defer final wording and voice choice. Do not burn POC hours on audio production.
  • Technician journeys and additional self-services: out of scope. Note them as roadmap, resist scope drift.

Evidence that strengthens a production decision

  • Intent accuracy measured on a JCI-supplied utterance set, reported per language.
  • Test-On completion rate across happy path and each exception branch, with transfer reasons.
  • Configuration change effort: time to add a rule, a prompt and a language, measured during the POC rather than asserted.
  • A production-readiness plan with the security, monitoring, scaling and change-management items §15 asks for.
  • A defensible effort and commercial view for both POC and production rollout — §15 requires it, and vagueness here reads as risk.

Questions for the Account Executive

Internal only. These determine whether we invest, and how.

Account and relationship

  • What is our history with Johnson Controls, and is there any existing Genesys footprint anywhere in the enterprise — any business unit, any region, any acquired entity?
  • How did this document reach us? Inbound request, competitive RFP, relationship, partner-led?
  • Who is Mario Navarro, and is he the evaluator, the author-on-behalf-of, or the incumbent's counterpart?
  • Do we know who else received this document?

Sponsorship and decision process

  • Who owns the business outcome, and who owns the platform decision? Are they the same person?
  • Is this a CX initiative, an IT consolidation initiative, or a cost exercise? The answer changes our entire narrative.
  • Is there a formal evaluation committee, scoring model or procurement process behind this?
  • Who has to say yes for a production purchase, and has that path ever been walked at JCI?

Why now

  • What triggered this? Contract renewal, a cost review, dissatisfaction, a new leader, a broader modernization program?
  • Why does a single self-service journey warrant a rebuild POC?
  • Where does Spanish come from — a specific market, a compliance requirement, a customer complaint, an executive request?
  • Is there a date anyone cares about? The document names none.

Competitive and incumbent

  • How healthy is the NICE relationship, and when does that contract come up?
  • Is Omilia still engaged, still delivering, still liked?
  • Is NICE proposing its own automation as the alternative to this POC?
  • Are Amazon, Google, Microsoft or a systems integrator in this conversation?

Commercial and strategy

  • Are we willing to invest SC and PS effort in a POC with no dated production decision attached?
  • Is the POC funded, or is it a free evaluation? What did we agree to, if anything?
  • What is the desired account outcome — self-service attach behind NICE, a beachhead, or a full platform displacement? Answer this before we design anything.
  • What is the realistic production footprint if we win, and does it justify the POC investment?

Politics

Section 9

Recommended AE Conversation Plan

A usable talk track for a 20-minute internal call. Outcome: two decisions and one owner list.

Opening statement

"I've been through the whole document. Two things stand out before we talk about the POC itself.

First, this is an Omilia design specification with the branding taken out — their terminology, their node numbers, their diagrams, and a couple of review comments they forgot to delete. We're being asked to rebuild a working application against a spec written by the people who built it. That's not a problem, but it means parity gets us nowhere. We have to change what they're measuring.

Second, and this is the one I need your read on: a document about one voice self-service journey asks us about predictive dialing, campaign compliance, chat, SMS and operational dashboards. Nobody needs a dialer to put an alarm panel on test. Either someone at JCI is quietly comparing platforms, or someone is building an internal case. Which is it, and what do we want out of this account?"

Discussion flow

  • 0–3 minThe two framing points above. Get the AE's reaction to the consolidation read.
  • 3–7 minAccount context: history, sponsor, origin of the request, why now, NICE contract timing, who else is bidding.
  • 7–11 minThe architecture decision. Bot behind NICE versus Genesys as the front door. Lay out effort, ceiling and competitive frame for each. Decide, or agree what we need to know to decide.
  • 11–15 minDelivery reality: no APIs, no test data, no dates, no numbers. Propose the mock-service approach and the dated-dependency list. Flag the Test-On rule complexity honestly.
  • 15–18 minWin strategy in three sentences: exception paths, bilingual in one journey, native reporting — plus co-authored measurable success criteria.
  • 18–20 minInvestment: what we'll commit, what we need from the AE, what has to be true before we build anything. Assign owners and dates.

The talking points to land

  1. We are responding to the incumbent's own design document. Parity is table stakes; the win is compression.
  2. The architecture choice — behind NICE or in front of it — is the deal, and it is ours to propose.
  3. Outbound and digital in a voice POC is the consolidation tell. It is also the only place a bot-only competitor has nothing to say.
  4. Spanish is new capability for JCI, not parity. Everyone else will underscope it. I need to verify our support before we promise it.
  5. No APIs, no test data, no dates. We solve that with a mock layer and a dated dependency list rather than a caveat.
  6. Their acceptance criteria have no numbers. Co-authoring measurable ones is a service to them and it moves the scoring onto our ground.
  7. Configuration migration is explicitly excluded. The incumbent's switching-cost argument is already gone — JCI removed it themselves.

Decisions to make together on this call

  1. Architecture posture. Do we propose Genesys as the voice front door, respond within the NICE-fronted frame, or present both with a recommendation?
  2. Investment level. Full SC build effort, or a scoped response with build contingent on a qualified production path?
  3. Scope discipline. Agree now that outbound and digital are demonstrated, not built.
  4. Deal team. Do we need PS input on effort, an AI specialist for AVA and bilingual, or a manager review before committing?
  5. Sequencing. Do we push for a discovery call before responding, or respond and use the response to earn the meeting?

Deliberately left open

  • Production sizing and commercial framing — no volumes exist yet.
  • Final ID&V design — depends on whether the four-value sequence is compliance-driven.
  • POC timeline — cannot be committed before API availability is dated.
  • Whether Test-Off and technician journeys enter the conversation.
Desired outcome

Architecture posture chosen, investment level agreed, scope boundaries set, discovery questions assigned to the AE, and my validation list closed before we write a line of the response.

Section 12

Useful Extensions

Secondary to the brief above, but worth the AE's attention.

Adjacent self-service the document points at without asking for

System Test-On implies System Test-Off, test extension and test status inquiry — the same site lookup, the same authorization, the same rule surface, and almost certainly the same callers. The baseline also references a technician-versus-customer caller distinction and a testcat_id value reserved for technician tests, which suggests a separate technician journey exists or is intended. None of this is in scope. All of it is cheap to add once the integration layer exists, and it is the natural second phase to put on the table when the production conversation starts.

Digital as a better fit than voice for parts of this journey

A technician standing at a panel scheduling a test window is a better candidate for SMS or web messaging than for a phone call — structured data entry, a written confirmation record, and an audit trail without transcription risk. The document asks us to recommend chat and SMS use cases; this is the recommendation, and it is more credible than a generic channel list because it comes from their own workflow.

Platform consolidation implications

If this business unit runs NICE plus Omilia, the AE should establish what the rest of Johnson Controls runs. A global enterprise with an alarm-monitoring central station operation almost certainly has other contact center estates — field service dispatch, building solutions support, regional operations. A single-journey POC in one business unit is either a narrow project or the visible edge of a much larger estate. Worth knowing which before deciding how much to invest. AE to map

Measurement as a product of the POC

Beyond passing acceptance criteria, the POC can leave JCI with something they do not have today: a baseline. Intent distribution from real utterances, containment by intent, exception frequency by rule, per-language performance. If we produce that, we are not a vendor who passed a test — we are the vendor who showed them their own operation. That is also the artifact most likely to survive into an internal business case we never see.

Stakeholders the AE may need beyond the author

  • The central station operations leader who owns handle time and agent capacity.
  • The IT or enterprise architecture owner of the NICE relationship.
  • Security and compliance, given PIC handling, recording and alarm-test audit records.
  • Whoever owns the Spanish-language requirement — they are a sponsor we have not identified.
  • Procurement, if this is heading toward a formal evaluation rather than a technical POC.

One more thing to watch

Watch item

The review comment in the baseline about not having the original diagrams, and about an AI tool burning time trying to recreate them, tells us the team preparing this evaluation is stretched. That is an opening. A vendor who reduces their workload — proposes the context contract, supplies the reason-code taxonomy, drafts measurable criteria, brings the mock services — becomes easy to work with at exactly the moment they need that. Do not mention the comment. Just be that vendor.
