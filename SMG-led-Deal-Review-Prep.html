M Metrics Partial — pain is quantified, business-value dollars are not Partial ▸
Definition

Quantifiable measures of value the solution provides — chiefly the business impact (M2): revenue increase, cost reduction, or risk mitigation.

GWoS: Qualify, Discover
What SMG will ask
  • What M1 customer stories have you shared with this account?
  • What M2 metrics have you uncovered — what's the business impact, in revenue, cost, or risk terms?
  • Has the Champion agreed to defend those outcomes in the business case?
Where we stand
  • Mario has volunteered two real numbers, unprompted: roughly 70% of the business unit's call volume is System Test-On, and the existing self-service system has plateaued around 20% containment after about two years.
  • Decision-criteria conversation has stayed at capability level (execution speed, journey quality, intuitiveness) — not yet translated into a dollar or headcount figure.
  • No M1 customer story or case study has been shared with JCI so far.
What's missing
  • Annual call volume, average handle time, cost per contact, false-dispatch volume and its cost, and a stated target containment number.
  • The combo/HVAC share of the monitored base — the number needed to correctly interpret the POC's eventual containment result.
  • Any M2 business-impact figure agreed with anyone, let alone a Champion committed to defending it.
Next best action

Ask directly for the volume/AHT/cost figures at the next technical touchpoint (bot demo or knowledge-transfer session), framed as "so we can tell you precisely what better looks like" rather than a data request for its own sake. Bring one relevant customer story as an M1 opener before the SMG review.

E Economic Buyer Gap — not identified across twelve touchpoints. The one to spend the most time on. Gap ▸
Definition

The person with overall authority in the buying decision. Signs off on the deal, and can say no even when every stakeholder has said yes.

GWoS: Discover
What SMG will ask
  • Who is the Economic Buyer? What's their title?
  • How do you know they're the Economic Buyer?
  • Have Decision Criteria and outcome metrics been reviewed with them?
Where we stand
  • Across twelve touchpoints spanning 8/24 through today (9/11) — six design sessions, three deep dives, three demos — nobody has behaved like a buyer.
  • Mario Navarro owns product and technology for the global contact center and is genuinely directive on design — but has explicitly stepped back to a backup role for the POC itself.
  • Day-to-day ownership has moved to Miguel Trenchs and Ulysses Parker, neither of whom carries EB-shaped authority.
What's missing
  • A name, a title, and a reason to believe that person can say yes or no on this deal.
Next best action

This is the highest-priority gap in the opportunity and the one to spend the most SMG time on. Use the POC-outcome delivery — Todd's team, expected the week of 9/15 — as the natural moment to request an executive-level readout. Loop Tom Nowinski in now on an AE-level EB strategy; the SC track can surface the need but can't close this alone.

Dc Decision Criteria Known — five criteria stated directly, thresholds still open to shape Known ▸
Definition

The criteria against which the decision to proceed with a solution will be judged.

GWoS: Develop
What SMG will ask
  • Have you discovered the technical criteria and the business impact behind them?
  • Have you or your Champion reviewed Decision Criteria with the Economic Buyer?
  • Has the criteria been influenced toward your differentiators? Has it locked out the competition?
Where we stand
  • Mario named five criteria directly: execution speed, customer journey quality, intuitiveness, containment versus agent routing, and success rate on qualifying alarms.
  • He went further and invited Genesys to help define what "acceptable," "good," and "excellent" look like on the testing schedule — an unusually open door at this stage.
  • New, today A second set of criteria may be forming on the infrastructure side — the architecture deep dive spent real time on multi-region redundancy, 4-nines SLA, and data-residency handling (China, Canada), none of which trace back to Mario's original five. Worth confirming whether this becomes a formal criterion or stays informational due diligence.
What's missing
  • The criteria haven't been reviewed with an Economic Buyer, because one hasn't been identified yet.
  • No confirmation the criteria are written in a way that favors Genesys specifically — right now they read as vendor-neutral capability statements, not ones that lock out NICE or the conversational incumbent.
  • No confirmation yet on whether the infrastructure-track questions (redundancy, SLA, data residency) roll up into the same decision, or belong to a separate IT approval track entirely.
Next best action

Take Mario up on his offer. Propose the acceptable/good/excellent thresholds in writing before the next testing block — written so Genesys's actual strengths (single-tenant breadth, native context carry, Copilot-assisted resolution) are what "excellent" looks like. Separately, ask Yeshi or Artem directly whether their architecture review feeds the same decision as Mario's, or a different one.

Get ahead of it rather than waiting for JCI to raise it late. Deal-shape risk read

Three ways this opportunity resolves, from the earlier SOW analysis. The current evidence pattern doesn't yet distinguish which one we're actually in.

ScenarioWhat it looks likeVerdict
A · Bot swap behind NICE Genesys becomes only the self-service layer; NICE keeps routing, reporting, and the agent desktop. Weakest possible ground — we inherit the integration seam, lose native reporting and context, and compete head-on against a vendor whose entire product is conversational capture. Avoid
B · Full stack consolidation Genesys Cloud CX absorbs routing, self-service, digital, outbound, and reporting into one platform. This is the deal the SOW actually describes, even though it never says so — every capability JCI asked us to prove is native here, and it's the only scenario where our single-tenant architecture is an advantage rather than a liability. Target
C · Price-discovery stalking horse A NICE/Omilia renewal is approaching, and this POC exists to move the incumbent's number rather than to switch. Cheap to test, expensive to miss — contract dates and the presence or absence of a named Economic Buyer would reveal this inside a single conversation. Qualify out early
Why this matters for the forecast category

Twelve touchpoints of deep technical engagement with zero commercial engagement is consistent with either B or C — the pattern alone can't tell them apart. The Economic Buyer gap is exactly the fact that would distinguish them. Until it closes, treat the forecast category as provisional rather than confirmed, and say so plainly in the review — that's a more defensible position than a confident number built on an unverified assumption.

Today's architecture deep dive shifts the read, but doesn't resolve it

A genuine global infrastructure conversation — new IT stakeholders, multi-region redundancy, data residency, a direct architecture teardown of NICE — is not what a narrow bot-swap or a pure price-discovery exercise usually produces. That leans toward Scenario B. But it's also exactly the kind of deep technical rabbit hole a stalled deal can disappear into without ever producing an Economic Buyer. Treat it as encouraging, not as resolution — the EB question is still the one that actually settles this.

Self risk assessment

In priority order. The first three are the hard MEDDPICC gaps — the rest are secondary but real.

1. No Economic Buyer identified

High — deal-blocking

Twelve touchpoints in — 6 design sessions, 3 deep dives, 3 demos — nobody has behaved like a buyer, and the closest candidate has stepped back to a backup role.

Mitigation: Use the week-of-9/15 POC-outcome delivery as the trigger for an executive-level readout request. Bring Tom Nowinski in explicitly on an AE-level EB strategy now, not after the review.

2. No compelling event or urgency driver

High

The containment pain is real and quantified, but nothing forces a decision by a date. A technically successful POC can sit indefinitely without one.

Mitigation: Ask directly, in the next technical session, whether there's a contract renewal, budget cycle, or executive commitment behind this — don't let the POC's own four-week window stand in for a real compelling event.

3. Paper Process unscoped on a life-safety function

Medium–high

No procurement, legal, or security gate has been named, and one is close to certain once a production conversation opens.

Mitigation: Proactively raise the Section 15 tracker's existing security/compliance recommendation at the next session — framed as de-risking JCI's own timeline, not as Genesys adding a gate.

4. Champion is unconfirmed, and the primary contact is in comparison mode

Medium

Mario is constructive but backup-role; Miguel, the current day-to-day contact, wasn't involved in writing the POC and is explicitly evaluating vendors — not champion behavior.

Mitigation: Test Mario directly at the next opportunity rather than assuming continued goodwill; don't build the plan around Miguel advocating internally.

5. Containment-scoring methodology could produce a misleading POC result

Medium — technical, but commercially consequential

A large share of the ~80 test scenarios are designed to end in a correct agent transfer, yet get scored against a stated containment criterion. Two different containment definitions — in-scope band vs. all-traffic — haven't been written down before testing starts.

Mitigation: Get both definitions documented in writing before JCI's testers score the scenarios, so a low headline number doesn't get read as a capability gap at the readout.

6. Two workstreams, no single owner connecting them

Medium — new as of today

Central Station Ops (Mario/Miguel/Ulysses/Darrell Wunderlich) and Global IT Infrastructure (Yeshi/Artem) are now both active, with no confirmed link between what either group needs in order to say yes. A deal can look busier and still not be closer to a decision if the two threads never merge into one buying process.

Mitigation: Ask directly — on the Genesys side and the JCI side — who is supposed to reconcile the infrastructure track's findings with the Central Station Ops decision. If the honest answer is "nobody yet," that's worth saying out loud at the SMG review rather than assuming it'll sort itself out.

System Test-On
The in-scope journey: a caller places a fire alarm or burglar alarm system on test ahead of scheduled maintenance. ~70% of the business unit's call volume.
Containment — two definitions in play
In-scope band: containment measured only within the automated portion of the journey. All-traffic: containment measured against every call to the line, including the ~80-scenario set designed to end in a transfer. These must be written down separately before testing is scored.
HVAC / combo
A modifier on monitored service types (BAHVAC, FAHVAC), not a peer category to fire alarm (FA) or burglar alarm (BA). JCI is also a major HVAC manufacturer, so the term carries product-line weight — don't phrase asks as if combo and HVAC are parallel categories.
PIC
Personal identification code — the caller-verification credential validated before any account action, including placing a system on test.
Global Media Fabric (GMF)
Genesys Cloud's mechanism for backhauling a call across its AWS backbone so it attaches to the lowest-latency (or a pinned) point of presence — e.g., a US-answered call routed to an agent in Frankfurt. Came up repeatedly in the 9/11 architecture call as the answer to JCI's global-routing questions.
China Security Law (CSL) review
A vendor security-review process JCI runs for China-facing deployments. JCI's side described an 8-hour CSL review already conducted on NICE, which produced a hard no on US-hosted AI models for China operations with narrow workarounds. No equivalent review has started for Genesys — see Paper Process.
"Wild West" (JCI's term)
Standard public internet for the last-mile telephony leg, as opposed to a dedicated/MPLS circuit. JCI has already decided internally to use it and is phasing out MPLS at two U.S. office locations — a network-topology decision made independently of the Genesys evaluation.
